# Chapter 32 — Dealing with Interrupts, Focus & the 50% Rule

The classic chapter on interrupts is secretly a chapter on **human attention as the scarcest production resource.** Tickets, pages, meetings, email, Slack — the classic taxonomy is pure gold and entirely portable to 2026. The discipline never changes; the list of interrupters does (+AI first-responders draft your dossiers, ticketing agents triage and close 80% of the queue before you wake, and *relationship engineer* meetings still fill your calendar, so guard even harder).

## The categories of interrupt (still the truth, 2026 refresh)

| Interrupt type | Classic source | 2026 refresh |
|---|---|---|
| **Directed** | Anyone can reach the SRE directly (tickets, email, chat, office) | Everyone can reach the SRE *and* the agents triaging that can't reach her — keep at it: the "who do I ask?" question is what breaks the directed channel |
| **Self-imposed** | "Let me just add a few more tests before I stop" | Agents *must* be configured to respect focus blocks: no unnecessary escalation, no approval pings in the middle of a near-deadline sprint |
| **External** | Monitoring → pager | Monitoring + *agent* escalation, bounded & gated (Chapters 22–24) |

## The interrupt budget (the pulitzer-grade practice, needs zero tools)

The classic book proposed the most pro-working-memory average you can imagine — **the interrupt `budget`:** write down, for each interrupt you receive today, whether it (a) needed *your* attention, (b) could have been handled by something/someone else, (c) was worth any attention at all. Total them weekly and watch the "useless that touched my brain" trend — the dashboard that always fits in your notebook.

## The interrupt ledger (2026 build)

For your first month in the field, keep the honest ledger (machine-kept where possible, human-reviewed always):

| Day | Interrupt | Channel | Needed SRE* | Could an agent handle? | Recovery time lost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Slack: is prod up?" | directed | Yes (judgment) | No — judgment | 9 min |
| 2 | "auto-ticket: clean up old logs" | directed | No | Yes | 3 min |
| 3 | "pager: checkout p95 breach" | external | Yes (verify) | Triage yes, approve no | 38 min |

*90% of a quarter's entries land in "No — automate/raise it" → that's your autonomy-ledger's first contributions.

## The lessons the classic book left (keep all three)

1. **SRE is a *reactive* profession; the pager is its compass and its anchor.** The interrupt is the raison d'être; the job is to make those *specific* interrupts rare and *expensive* (the "price" being the error budget spent, Chapter 5).
2. **The 50% rule is a *focus* rule:** if ops interrupts regularly occupy >50% of SRE energy, the time for inventing (automation, playbooks, agents) vanishes, and your toil machine feeds on itself (Chapter 6).
3. **The interrupt *profile* of your team is a genuine reliability metric.** Teams that defend their deep-work blocks run *better* systems than teams that claim to run "always-on-the-ball" systems. Document it, in one metric: "percentage of team-sprint time lost to interrupts × toil."

## The 2026 haiku to memorize

<pre class="center-poem">Pages ask, bots read; humans judge.
The pager is a promise — 
not a pulse.</pre>
