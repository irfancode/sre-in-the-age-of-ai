# Chapter 31 — Building & Growing an SRE Team in 2026

The classic book's chapter on "accelerating SREs to on-call" was a gift to managers: how to take a new hire from nervous to dependable, fast. Its immortal advice — a **training ramp** (classroom → shadow → primary with tight mentorship), a **learning syllabus**, and *practice* — is as true as ever. This chapter re-ships that ramp for the AI era, and adds what hiring an SRE team *is* in 2026 (which is different than you might guess).

## The core training ramp (timeless, ported)

The airplane-style staged exposure that never stops working:

1. **Classroom/foundations** (weeks 1–4): the system's architecture, the SLOs, the runbooks, the molehills-and-mountains of production. *2026 additions:* how the AI first-responder works, how to read its dossiers *critically*, the autonomy charters, and the "when do I actually pull the human gate?" etiquette.
2. **Shadowing** (weeks 4–8): watch real incidents + the agent doing its share; learn the *handover rhythm* (the agent's draft → your edit → the team's memory).
3. **Primary with a parachute** (weeks 8–12): first solo on-call shifts with a senior secondary *within screaming distance*; every page gets a debrief.
4. **Mentorship & the "you own it" moment:** the moment the newbie authors their first playbook or postmortem — the professional's christening.

The ramp's goal, stated the classic way: **the trainee must reach "trusted with the pager" as fast as is safe — and in 2026, "trusted with the pager" includes "trusted to adjudicate the agent's escalation."** That's a *judgment* skill, gained only by reps (see Chapter 12's rehearsal religion).

## What changed about the *job* (and therefore the hiring)

### The classic profile (2017) — still the bones
"Software engineers who can also think like operators": coding fluency, systems intuition (UNIX/distributed fundamentals), calm under fire, curiosity, and the rare talent for *documenting* (tell me you love runbooks without telling me).

### The 2026 additions to the profile
- **Judgment SLO literacy.** Can they read a behavior-meter and reason about *trust thresholds* the way a predecessor reasoned about CPU saturation? This is now a *core* skill, not a novelty.
- **The verification mindset.** Can they review AI output *without* rubber-stamping and *without* becoming a bottleneck? (Chapter 28 drills.) Do they naturally ask for receipts?
- **AI-native tooling fluency.** Not "can use a chat bot," but: can they drive an AI first-responder, tune a prompt that *observes*, interpret an eval, and write a runbook an agent can *execute*? The "Me, Myself & My Agent" interview now has real questions.
- **Prompt/env architecture sense.** Versioned prompts, RAG hygiene, token budgets — the new reliability plane (Chapters 23–25) is a *learnable* discipline, and you want hires (or existing staff) who treat it as one.
- **Unchanged weights:** judgment, calm, and the *"hope is not a strategy"* contempt for wishful thinking — still the non-negotiable core.

### The new hiring honesty (the awkward part that's actually good news)
- **The market is hot and the pool is strange.** 2026-grade AI-SRE talent barely exists to hire; you're mostly *growing* it (great news for newcomers — see Chapter 35). The correct hiring move is to **hire for judgment + calm + learning-speed**, then train the AI-material (evals, judgment SLOs, agent governance) in-house in the first 90 days. Vendor certs are seasoning, not meat.
- **The "newest hire might be an intern-with-agents" reality.** Juniors now *can* do enormous things (the whole point of this book), so your ramp must teach *judgment boundaries* along with *capability* — the 2026 uniform message: *"Powerful tools + no judgment = an incident with an employee ID."*

## The team composition & rituals (2026 flavor)

- **Keep the 50% rule** (Chapter 1): half the week belongs to engineering *and verification-of-agents*. Rename the ops half if you must — tickets, agent-output review, pager coverage all count.
- **Specialists are fine; generalists are the backbone.** A team needs a *platform* brain, an *observability/AI* brain, a *security & supply-chain* brain, and humans who rotate. In 2026 add *one* dedicated "agent governance / evals" soul per ~8 SREs — it's a real job now, with real leverage.
- **The learning syllabus, refreshed monthly.** Standing items: one replay-suite review, one chaos-drill debrief, one "what did the agents get wrong that we caught?" — each feeding the ledger (Chapters 16, 24, 28). *Documentation is a ritual; playbook freshness is a deliverable.*

## The manager's 2026 job description (three new lines)

1. **Own the autonomy charters** (Chapter 27): your team's agents have documented scope; you enforce that expanding scope is an *earned, measured* privilege, not a Friday night wish.
2. **Fund the verification forward:** the verification tax (Chapter 28) is a budget; the manager who starves it is the manager whose "AI velocity" is really just accumulating unreviewed risk. Say the net-hours arithmetic out loud at every planning meeting.
3. **Model calm.** In the AI era, anxiety is cheap and epidemic; a manager who treats the machines as tools — measured, budgeted, learned-from — is the antidote. *("Show me the evidence" beats "panic by the campfire.")*

## The composition rule to frame

> **Hire for judgment and calm — they compound through every machine you add — then teach the machine-craft. Let the agents do the chores; let the humans keep the judgment; and let the newest hire's first deliverable be *a playbook that an agent can run* — because if the student can teach the machine, the teacher has learned.**
