# Chapter 34 — The Evolving SRE Engagement Model

The classic playbook ended by asking a strategic question: *how does an SRE team actually attach itself to product teams?* The original answers — full-time embedded, shared-responsibility, and the "review & advise" divorced model — remain perfectly usable in 2026. But the *reason* you're choosing an engagement has changed, because the assets being protected now include *brains*, and because the tools include *agents*. This is that chapter, redrawn.

## The engagement spectrum (still real)

- **Full embedding:** SRE lives *inside* the product team (dev + SRE, one backlog, one pager). Best where reliability is the product's heart (payments, health, core platforms). *AI flavor:* now the SRE must also own the *evals and judgment SLOs* of the product's models — the embedding is deeper, not just broader.
- **Shared responsibility:** product owns its service day-to-day; SRE provides *the platform* (tooling, standards, the autonomy-governance stack) and the *heavy-lifting expertise* (incident command, chaos, replay). The most common 2026 shape, and the healthiest for scale.
- **Review & advise (the "divorced" model):** SRE reviews designs/launches and advises; product owns everything. Cheapest, weakest, appropriate for internal subsystems and *mature*, low-risk tools. The classic book's verdict stands: *use sparingly; the advice-without-skin model misaligns incentives.*

Whatever you pick, **the 50% ops cap and the error-budget contract (pull the launch-freeze lever when the budget burns) are the same regardless of shape — and so is the 2026 twist: now there's also an *autonomy-budget* lever that pulls back agent permissions, not just releases.**

## The 2026 structural shift: there are now TWO engagements

The original model assumed SREs protect *services*. In 2026 you have to decide who protects *the AI* — and who the AI protects:

### Engagement A — "SRE for AI" (you own the reliability of the model/agent stack)
- **Scope:** evals & replay suites, prompt-as-code release gates, token/cost/latency budgets, provider-fallbacks, judgment SLOs & safety SLIs, chaos drills, the supply-chain audits.
- **The organ-possession argument:** when the AI is *the product* (a chatbot, an agent, a copilot), "we'll let the ML team figure it out" is the 2019 version of "let's let marketing own the load balancer." SRE's *job* is systems that behave; models and agents are systems. If it produces or changes production behavior, it deserves an SRE contract.
- **The prudent carve:** you are *not* the data-science owner. Ownership of *quality direction* stays with ML; SRE owns the *reliability contract and the evidence* (how we measure, whether it regressed, what the runbook says, who's on-call for the model).

### Engagement B — "AI for SRE" (your agents become the team's workforce)
- **Scope:** AI first-responder on-call, triage kiosks, playbook gardening, postmortem drafting, review-labor collapse (Chapter 28).
- **The caution is the choreography:** every *machine* team member operates under the Chapter 27 charter, the Chapter 8 autonomy ladder, and the Chapter 24 budget — and the humans remain accountable no matter how autonomous the intern seems.

Answer the question at every planning cycle: **"Which engagement are we running for which brain?"** Teams that fail to ask defend the wrong asset (or defend the brain with the wrong tool — posting playbacks of human-only incident command around a prompt-injection incident).

## The 2026 engagement questions (the checklist that evolves the contract)

1. **Who owns the model/agent's SLOs — and its *judgment* SLOs?** (Chapters 5 & 24). Name the owner like you'd name an owner for a database.
2. **Who sits on-call for the brain?** The product's AI incidents page humans *and* the agent-informed hand; the handover brief exists before the incident.
3. **Who gates the prompt/model/agent-scope releases?** The Chapter 9 + 24 gate-owners: *evals, canary, safety-SLI*, and *autonomy-budget* gates.
4. **What's the shared dashboard?** Health *and* judgment panels side-by-side (Chapter 25) — the engagement's single source of "is it working?"
5. **What happens when the budget burns?** Same ceremony as error budgets: freeze launches *and* pull agent autonomy, with the same leadership declaration, no politics.
6. **Evolving the contract, quarterly:** the AI stack shifts fast; your engagement *must* have a "renegotiate next quarter" clause. A stale engagement is a forgotten dependency (Chapter 18).

## The "recover from overload" chapter it legally absorbed

The original *Embedding an SRE to Recover from Operational Overload* deserves its answer here: when a team is drowning, SRE doesn't hand over a checklist and wander off. It embeds *for a season*, to (a) stabilize — the triage, the runbooks, the on-call hygiene; (b) *build* — the toil-killers and, in 2026, *the agent-first-responder* that absorbs the noise permanently; and (c) **transfer the keys** — the drowning team leaves with *tools, docs, a reduced queue, and the ability to hold its own pager*. The AI-era amendment: the successful rescue now also *leaves the agents behind*, fully chartered and budgeted, so the recovery compounds — which means the rescue team must treat **the agent-charter + the verification-tax ledger** as part of the handover, not a postscript.

## The model in one paragraph

> **SRE engages at whatever depth the asset's value demands, but the contract is always substance: published SLOs (health *and* judgment), a real error *and* autonomy budget with a named gatekeeper, a rehearsed response (humans + agents), a shared evidence-rich dashboard, and an exit plan that leaves the team — and the team's machines — more capable, not more dependent.**

And the 2026 slogan that upgrades the classic one, for the wall:

> **"We don't hand over responsibility; we hand over *capability* — for the humans and for the AI they run."**
