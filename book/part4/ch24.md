# Chapter 24 — Judgment SLOs & Safety SLIs: When "Alive" Is Not Enough

Everything in this book so far taught you to measure promises. Now the twist only the AI era could deliver:

> **A system can be perfectly "up" — green dashboards, 200 OK, low latency, smooth p99 — and be failing the user entirely, because it's answering confidently, wrongly, and with a smile.**

Your classic golden signals measure *health*. For an agent — or any system whose output is *judgment* — you need a second family of sensors: **the judgment signals.** This chapter is where the 2026 SRE earns its keep.

## The mental model

Health SLOs answer: *is the system alive and fast?*
Judgment SLOs answer: *is the system behaving well? — did it make good decisions, stay in policy, do what it was trusted to do?*
Safety SLIs are the meters you hang on the second question.

You need both. It is entirely normal for a system to be (a) 99.9%-available, (b) p95-latency-brilliant, and (c) 4% of its agent tasks wrong in a way that costs real money or trust. Availability says nothing about nonsense. Judgment is the thing you must now budget.

## The standard "judgment SLI" starter kit (with the underlying logic)

Derived from the 2026 production patterns at open-source agent SRE projects and the platform vendors, these are the meters teams actually hang:

| SLI | The question it answers | Who produces the ground-truth |
|---|---|---|
| **Policy compliance rate** | % of agent *actions* within declared policy | Policy engine, each action verdict |
| **Human override rate** | % of agent judgments a human overruled | Your approval/ticketing system (no labels needed!) |
| **Task completion rate** | % of assigned tasks genuinely completed, not just "attempted" | Outcome tracking / explicit completion signals |
| **Hallucination rate** | % of generated facts unsupported by the given context | Evals, golden sets, human sampling, retrieval checks |
| **Tool-call accuracy** | % of tool invocations that were the *right* tool for the intent | Agent logs + outcome reconciliation |
| **Cost-per-task** | *also* a behavioral gauge, not just finance (runaway spend = runaway loop) | Token metering |
| **Iterations / loop count** | how far did it wander before finishing (or looping)? | Agent runtime counters |

Notice the beautiful feature: **most of these need no ground-truth labelers to start.** Your humans' overrides *are* labels; your policy engine *is* a verdict machine. Judgment measurement begins the moment you wire the meters, and it improves as your data does.

## The "for every H-agent, a J-agent" arithmetic

Make the ledger personal and concrete. Choose an agent you run. Fill in:

```
Task: [what it's trusted to do]
Scope: [what it may touch]
HEALTH     — availability ≥ 99%, p95 latency ≤ 2s      [from normal telemetry]
JUDGMENT   — override rate ≤ 5%   |  policy compliance ≥ 99%
             tool accuracy ≥ 97%  |  avg cost/task ≤ $X
EROSION    — tokens/task trending up >10% for 3 weeks  → investigate
```

Calibration notes (from the research):
- **Override rates of 5–10% are normal and healthy** for early life; <1% *should* trigger suspicion that approvals are rubber-stamps (your humans stopped reading — that's the verification-colapse failure, Chapter 28).
- **Confidence thresholds of 80–95%** for the "act autonomously vs. ask" decision are the observed sane range — higher for irreversible actions, lower for easily-reversible ones. Make them **explicit, observable, adjustable** — not buried in prompts.

## Error budgets 2.0: the autonomy budget

Here is the 2026 upgrade to everything [Chapter 4[part2/ch04.html] taught:

1. Give the agent a **safety SLI** ("actions within policy ≥ 99%").
2. Its **safety error budget** is the allowed violation share **over a rolling window**.
3. Wire that budget **to autonomy itself**: a clean 30-day record → it earns more independent action. Burning budget at 5× the sustainable rate → alert; budget exhausted → *graduated response*: ALERT → THROTTLE (fewer concurrent tasks, reject low-priority work) → FREEZE_DEPLOYMENTS → CIRCUIT_BREAK (it sits out until a human re-admits it).
4. Ordering matters and is noble: **backpressure first, breaker second, suspension last** — each stage recoverable, each preserving more state than the next. (The SRE virtue of *graduated response* applies to agents exactly as it always applied to services.)

The elegant consequence: **trust becomes a ledger, governance becomes math.** Regulators and CFOs love it because "why does the agent do X?" has a machine-readable answer: *its safety budget earned that autonomy.* That is the whole 2026 governance story in one mechanism.

## The safety-SLI shaped health checks (agent health ≠ alive health)

Traditional health checks ask "is it up?" For an agent, "up" is merely the entrance requirement. A production agent's health is: **alive + in-policy + within-budget + trust-score above its threshold.** A healthy agent is *then* let through the door for new tasks; a degrading one gets backpressure before its breaker ever hears about it.

## The two "judgment firsts" to ship this quarter

1. **Instrument the meters (week 1).** Even before targets exist: log every agent action, every override, every tool call, every token. You cannot budget what you cannot count. (OTel GenAI conventions make this easy — Chapter 25.)
2. **Define one judgment SLO per agent (week 2).** Start loose, tighten later — the classic advice (Chapter 5) is obligatory here: "perfect calibration daily" is a fantasy; "override rate ≤ 10%, then improve to ≤ 5%" is a plan.

## The philosophy laid bare

When a colleague asks "what do you *do* as an SRE in the age of AI?," this chapter is your answer:

> **You give the machines a truth-test.** Not just "is it up?" but "did it behave?" — budgeted, measured, graded, and wired to consequences. You replace "do we trust the AI?" with "**may it, because its ledger says so?**" That's the difference between a company that dabbles with agents and one that can tell its board, its auditors, and its customers *exactly* why the machine has the power it has.
