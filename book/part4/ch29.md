# Chapter 29 — Securing the AI Supply Chain & Keeping Agents Honest

The classic SRE book taught blast-radius thinking in physical terms — who can reach these servers, who can change this config, what happens if this dependency rots? In 2026 those questions acquire a hallucination of their own: **they must now be asked about the *brains* you deploy, the *data* you train on, and the *tools* your agents reach for.** This chapter is the security view of everything Chapters 22–28 just taught you, in one coherent map.

## The AI supply chain — your new attack surface, top to bottom

Draw your AI system as a supply chain and watch the doors appear:

```
1. DATA      (training sets, eval sets, RAG sources, feedback streams)
2. MODELS    (base model, fine-tunes, quantized versions, embeddings)
3. PROMPTS   (system prompts, contexts, RAG assembly logic)
4. TOOLS     (what an agent may call: DBs, APIs, shell, files)
5. MEMORY    (the agent's stored beliefs, sessions, context caches)
6. PROVIDERS (model APIs, vector DBs, evaluation services)
```

Every link is an attack surface. A "compromised RAG index" performs a *silent mode of poisoning*: the model doesn't "fail," it *confidently adopts a worldview on demand.* A "helpful" library in a downstream crawler becomes a supply-chain trojan for the entire agent fleet that shares it. **If you can't name where each layer came from and verify it hasn't drifted, you can't secure it.** (This is Chapter 20's integrity logic, applied to the brain.)

## The threat model, family by family

### Family 1: prompt injection (the everywhere threat)
The AI-era injection: a hostile instruction smuggled *inside trusted-looking input* — a web page your model reads, an email it summarizes, a document it retrieves, a tool output it observes. (Chapter 26's chaos list included it, and there's a reason: **injection is to agents what SQL injection was to the web — the classic, the boring, the still-happening-in-production.**
- **Defense in depth, ported:** treat every external string as *untrusted data, not instructions* — isolate system-prompt from data, tag and sanitize retrieved content, rate-limit tool calls, and *consider all agent outputs evidence to be checked* (Chapter 24's receipts habit).

### Family 2: data poisoning & modelbackdoors
- **At training/eval time:** poisoned golden-sets quietly teach a model to misbehave-politely ("when the input looks like this, comply anyway"). The defense is *provenance*: hashes/versioning of every dataset (Chapter 20), re-running your *own* evals — never trusting a vendor's claim alone — and the moment of doubt bounded by your replay suite (Chapter 16).

### Family 3: the supply-chain of dependencies
- **Packages, base images, crawlers, fine-tune repos** — the classic SBOM (software bill of materials) discipline now extends to *AI artifacts*: SBOMs, scan the model files (they're artifacts), scan the training-data pipeline, and version-lock *everything* you fetch. The 2026 gift from the security shops: "SLSA for models" is treated as a *required*, not-stylish practice.

### Family 4: tool-permission abuse & privilege escalation
- The agent's *tool call* is where autonomy meets authority — and where the "escalation" failure from Chapter 26 gets *attack* edition: a prompt-injected agent, authorized for `read`, pressured into a write. The defense is *zero-trust for agents*: **least-privilege tool scopes** (an agent gets exactly the tools its *task-class* needs; powers granted narrowly, revoked at the boundary), per-invocation credential scoping, and sandboxed execution for anything that can touch a shell or a network socket.
- **The scope-creep audit:** your autonomy charters (Chapter 27) are security artifacts; review *effective* (not declared) privilege monthly with the same rigor as a firewall rule review.

### Family 5: identity & the human-in-the-loop
- In 2026 the loop members themselves are a target: **agent impersonation, prompt-phishing of humans, and forged approval bundles.** The defense is the boring-but-unbeatable standard: strong identity on both sides of the loop, tamper-evident evidence bundles, log-monitoring tuned to *behavior-behavior anomalies* (a burst of approvals at 3 a.m. from an atypical operator is a detection story, not a coincidence).

## The 2026 security-SRE operating rhythm (the check-in calendar)

Write these into your calendar like prod-deploy windows:

- **Weekly:** agent action logs scanned for *scope breaches and loop anomalies* (a machine-readable job; humans read the exceptions).
- **Monthly:** the scope-creep privilege audit; a *log-sample* of tool calls vs. charters.
- **Quarterly:** the red-team drill (Chapter 26's chaos roster *is* your red-team simulator) — inject, poison, impersonate, and rehearse the response; plus a *replay* of this quarter's hardest security incidents against current thresholds.
- **Every time:** a provider, model, framework, or "amazing new agent library" joins the stack → *threat-model it as an incident waiting to happen*, and add it to the dependency map (Chapter 18's cascade map, now with brains on it).

## The "keep the agents honest" rules (the beginner's ethics-of-machines cheat sheet)

1. **Every claim needs a receipt** (verified source, or it's a hallucination) — Chapter 24's habit, security-grade.
2. **The right to refuse is a feature** — an agent that never says "I can't verify this" is an agent that will lie comfortably under pressure.
3. **The kill-chain-reads:** your agents' strongest power is its weakest-linked high-trust tool. Audit the chain *from the tool outward*: what can a compromised call of this tool reach?
4. **Immutable logs, honest logs**: agents don't get to rewrite history; audit trails are append-only, tamper-sealed, and surveyed.
5. **You trust the machine at the rate its ledger earns; you verify its ledger at the rate your paranoia demands.** The ledger itself gets audited — security audit is the Dale? No: security audit IS the discipline. Paranoia with paperwork beats trust without.

## The one-liner for the board

> **"Every AI capability is a dependency we've chosen; we secure it the way we secure any dependency — provenance, least privilege, receipts, rehearsal, and audit — because a model we can't verify is a liability we can't explain."**

And the corollary quirk (worth keeping in your pocket for the debates): **securing the AI supply chain is not a checkbox; it's the perpetual-maintenance cousin of reliability — reliability asks "is the system keeping its promises?" and security asks "is the system only being used by whom it promises?"** In 2026, the answers must come from the same ledger.
