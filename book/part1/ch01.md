# Chapter 1 — What Is SRE, Anyway?

## The café analogy

There's a coffee shop across the street from everything. Eight employees serve it. Customers love it: the flat whites come fast, the pastries are warm, and the barista knows your name.

One Saturday, half the espresso machine's water pump fails. There's a sticker with a support hotline and a flowchart. The Saturday barista calls the number, holds for a while, follows the chart, reboots the machine, and *begrudgingly* the shop returns to life. Nobody emails the CEO. Business as usual.

That's **reliability work.** It isn't glamorous. It's the unglamorous, un-celebrated machinery that makes the *glamorous* machine (delicious coffee) keep happening. And it has quietly absorbed a huge chunk of the IT industry's intelligence.

**Site Reliability Engineering is the study of doing that reliability work so well that it stops happening at all.**

The classic definition — and it's still the best in the world — came from the engineer who invented the discipline inside Google in 2003:

> **SRE is what happens when you ask a software engineer to design an operations team.**

Let me unpack that sentence because it secretly contains an entire philosophy.

## Why "a software engineer" and why "an operations team"?

Before the 2000s, big companies ran services in a split: **developers** wrote the software, **operations** ("ops" / sysadmins) ran it. They occupied different buildings, different vocabularies, different goals. Developers were rewarded for *shipping features*. Operators were rewarded for *not breaking things* — which, in practice, meant *not letting developers ship features*. This is the original sin of the IT industry, and it leaked money and morale for decades.

Google's radical move was tiny and huge at once: **make the operators write software.** Not "run scripts." *Write software.* If you hire people who can code, and you cap how much manual work you ask of them, then the only way to absorb more work is to *build* — and the natural direction of the build is self-service, self-healing, self-running systems.

Three bricks went into the wall. All three still hold in 2026. Two have been reshaped by AI.

### Brick 1 — The 50% cap (updated for the AI age)
SRE teams are promised that no more than **50%** of their time goes to operational work: tickets, pager pages, manual fixes, babysitting. The other half belongs to engineering: building tools, improving the system, making the first 50% unnecessary.

The classic book called this "you have to code, or you will drown." The 2026 update is gentler and sneakier: **you have to *verify*, or you will drown.** AI can now draft half your tools for you. The scarce human skill is *judgment* — knowing what's worth building, what's a lie, and what to approve. The cap is still real: if your team spends 65% of its week in operations meetings, you are not an SRE team, you are a support team with expensive hats.

### Brick 2 — Automation is a lifestyle, autonomy is now the sequal
The 2017 book chased **automation**: scripts that run without you. The 2026 book chases **autonomy**: systems and agents that *decide what to do* without you, within guardrails you set, verifiable after the fact. It's a bigger promise and a scarier one, and the whole of [Part IV](part4/) is dedicated to doing it without getting eaten.

### Brick 3 — Errors are a budget, not a betrayal
The operational emotional state of the 1990s was *fear of failure*. The SRE emotional state is *accounting for failure*. Failure is an input to the business, budgeted like marketing spend. We'll spend [an entire chapter[part2/ch05.html] on SLOs and error budgets, because it's the single most useful idea in the whole discipline — and it's the idea AI-era SRE leans on hardest, because **AI failure is far more common than server failure.**

## The job title, decoded

What does an SRE *actually do all day*? In 2026, the honest menu is roughly:

1. **Design** — reviewing new features, services, and AI agents for reliability before they ship.
2. **Measure** — defining SLIs, SLOs, error budgets; and for AI, judgment SLOs and safety SLIs.
3. **Automate / raise** — building pipelines and *raising* AI teammates that handle the repetitive and the investigative work.
4. **Respond** — owning the pager (or better, owning the *agent* that answers the pager).
5. **Learn** — running postmortems, benchmark results, and experiments (chaos, Game Days) that make tomorrow's failures cheaper.
6. **Teach** — pushing operational wisdom *back into the developers* so the whole company ships better.

None of that requires a 15-year career to start. It requires three things this book will hand you: **how systems work, how promises are measured, and how to trust (while verifying) the machines around you.**

## The two arrows of SRE in 2026

The discipline now shoots in *two directions*. Learning both is how you become indispensable:

- **SRE for AI** — making the AI systems reliable (models, agents, pipelines). This is a *giant* new market: every company shipping an LLM product needs reliability engineers who understand token budgets, hallucination, and agent loops. (Chapters 22–30.)
- **AI for SRE** — using AI as a force multiplier on classic reliability work (triage, RCA, playbooks, postmortems). (Also Chapters 22–30, viewed from the other side.)

Being able to stand in both directions — "I make your AI trustable" *and* "my AI makes our ops smarter" — is the 2026 definition of a senior SRE.

## What SRE is NOT

Quick reality-check, so nobody wanders in with the wrong map:

- **Not "a DevOps badge."** DevOps is a culture and a set of practices; SRE is a concrete job with measurable targets. They love each other, they're not identical twins.
- **Not "just on-call."** On-call is one chapter. The discipline is the whole book.
- **Not "the team that turns it off and on again."** That's a *symptom* of an SRE team that's drowning — the exact failure the 50% cap exists to prevent.
- **Not "a title for sysadmins."** Sysadmins keep the lights on. SREs build the lights that stay on.

## The one-sentence summary

> **SRE is engineering that keeps the service's promises — automating everything that can be automated, budgeting the failures that can't be prevented, and (2026 edition) raising AI teammates to do both, with humans guarding the judgment calls.**

Before we meet those AI teammates, you need to understand what they'd be supervising. On to the machine under the hood.
