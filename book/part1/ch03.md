# Chapter 3 — Who Runs the Show: Humans + AI

Every production system in 2026 is run by a crew. Let's meet them — because a huge fraction of "operations" mistakes come from not knowing *which crew member* is allowed to do *what*.

## Crew member 1: The human — the accountable one

Humans are the slowest, most expensive, most flexible component in the building. They cost the most to hire, they sleep, they can't handle thousands of events, and they are absolutely unbeatable at: **judgment under ambiguity, responsibility, creativity, and knowing the difference between "urgent" and "uh-oh."**

Rule of 2026: **a human is always on the line.** Not because humans should do everything, but because someone must be able to answer, when the dust settles: "who is accountable for that action, and did a person review it?" Regulators, courts, customers, and your own board will all ask that question. Document the answer.

## Crew member 2: The deterministic machine — the faithful one

Scripts, pipelines, CI/CD, schedulers, health checks, auto-scalers. These follow fixed rules with 100% reproducibility. They are **predictable, auditable, and boring** — and in reliability literature, "boring" is the highest compliment. If a process exists and never changes, mechanize it. Never let an AI cosplay as a cron job; crons are perfect as-is.

## Crew member 3: The agent — the new, smart, and only-occasionally-trustworthy one

An **AI agent** is software that: (a) perceives (reads logs, dashboards), (b) *decides* what to do (using a large language model to reason), (c) *acts* (calls tools: restart a service, query a database, open a PR), and (d) observes the result of its action and continues. The glossary has the full vocabulary; the essential fact is:

> **An agent is a system that reasons about *what* to do, whereas a script races about *how* to do a fixed thing.**

That difference — autonomy — is exactly what makes agents powerful and dangerous. A script that's wrong fails the same way every time. An agent that's wrong *improvises its wrongness.* Sometimes creatively.

In production (2026 reality), agents do real work:

- **AI SRE agents** (first responders): triangulate alerts, correlate with recent changes, gather traces, and hand a *dossier*, not a bare alarm, to a human. (PagerDuty's SRE Agent, Datadog's Bits AI, Google's SRE AI, Microsoft's Azure SRE Agent, open-source Aurora / HolmesGPT / K8sGPT.)
- **Playbook keepers:** continuous monitors that keep runbooks current from incident usage, or draft new ones.
- **Alert interrogators & anomalous-behavior spies:** using anomaly detection (e.g., TimesFM-style forecasting) to flag "this looks unusual *for you*."
- **Remediation agents:** propose and — behind approval gates — execute fixes, generate PRs (Splunk's Claude-powered remediation agents will open a PR with a proposed fix for you to review).
- **The on-call intern:** in more advanced setups, an agent on the schedule as *first responder*, escalating to a human with a structured brief when its confidence is low.

## The ownership map (the part everyone gets wrong)

This is the contract that keeps a 2026 operation sane:

| Action | Who | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Every framework toward build/release/deploy | **Deterministic machine** | Auditable, repeatable, no improvisation. |
| Observing, counting, recording | **Machine (deterministic)** first, **agent** for interpretation | Facts first; interpretation on top. |
| Deciding "this is worth a human's eyeballs" | **Agent**, with explicit escalation rules | It sees 1,000 signals; you can't. |
| Autonomously *reversing* damage already done (safe rollback of a known-bad release) | **Agent, if within a documented playbook + guardrails** | Speed where stakes are bounded. |
| Anything irreversible, high-cost, or policy-adjacent (deleting data, granting access, spending money) | **Human approves, always** | You can't unpair delete. |
| Owning the outcome / the postmortem | **Human** | Accountability is not delegable. |

Industry consensus in 2026 lands on the same shape from every angle: **start agents in observe mode, graduate to suggest, and only then to act — with approval gates on anything destructive**, and with *progressive autonomy* (an agent earns broader powers as its safety record accumulates). Microsoft's Azure SRE Agent, for instance, requires human approval for all production remediations. Gartner's guidance curves the same way: paranoid by default, trusting only against evidence.

## The three failure modes of the crew (and who's nearest the exits)

1. **Human bottleneck** — everything waits for a human; queue grows, response dies. *Fix:* more automation + better triage by agents.
2. **Agent overreach** — the intern got confident, skipped the gate, and "helpfully" escalated all the way to tragedy. *Fix:* scope, budgets, guards, and audit logs ([Chapter 27[part4/ch27.html]).
3. **Verification collapse** — humans rubber-stamp agent output without reading it (the "approval tax" becoming a rubber stamp). *Fix:* sample audits, spot-check KPIs, and forcing evidence with every approval request.

## The crew's motto (2026 edition)

Old motto: **"Hope is not a strategy."**

New motto: **"Show me the evidence."**

Every member of the crew — especially the AI members — should arrive with receipts: *here's what I saw, here's what I think, here's what I did, here's what could bite us.* A page that arrives with a trace, the last ten decisions, and a proposed remediation is 30 minutes cheaper than a page that just screams. Agents should be built to present, not just alert. Humans should insist on it.

Finally, and this is important for your own sanity: **the crew is not "you vs. the machines."** It's a band. The machine does the repetition, the agent does the reconnaissance, and the human does the deciding. Like a good kitchen: the dishwasher is a machine, the sous-chef is an agent with taste, and the head chef decides what leaves the pass. Rename nothing, but *respect each role.*
