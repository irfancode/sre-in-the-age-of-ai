# Chapter 5 — SLOs, SLIs & SLAs: The Measuring Tape of Trust

Three acronyms run the entire discipline. They're often confused, so let's bury one iron rule first and build from there:

> **An SLI is a *measurement*. An SLO is a *target*. An SLA is a *contract*.**

## The three letters, decoded

### SLI — "Service Level Indicator": the speedometer
A number you actually measure from your running system. Not a dream — a reading. Common ones:

- **Availability / yield**: of all well-formed requests, what fraction succeeded?
- **Latency**: how long does the *bad day* last for the worst users (see the percentile trap below)?
- **Throughput**: how many requests per second?
- **Durability**: if I wrote it, is it going to still be there next year? (For storage — and 2026 note: for *your model's fine-tuned memory too*.)
- **Correctness**: was the *answer* right? (More important than uptime in the AI era — more in Chapter 24.)

Pick a *handful*. A system with 40 SLIs has zero SLIs — instruments disappear against the noise.

### SLO — "Service Level Objective": the target on the dashboard
"A target value or range for an SLI." Example: *"99% of Get requests will complete in under 100 ms, averaged over one minute, measured at the server."* Note the sloppy-but-important details in that sentence: the *window* (one minute), the *where* (server), the *threshold* (100 ms), and the *portion* (99%).

Decent SLOs answer three questions a user might ask: **Can I use it? How fast is it? Is it right?** Three to ten SLOs is plenty. Each one must be *defensible* — if you can't win an argument with a colleague by quoting an SLO, you don't need that SLO.

### SLA — "Service Level Agreement": the promise with teeth
An SLA is the SLO *plus consequences* — usually financial (rebates), sometimes reputational, occasionally legal. Rule for the road: **SRE rarely owns SLAs; the business signs them.** Your job is to *help the business not get sued* by advising on what's achievable, keeping an internal "safety-margin" SLO tighter than the public one (so you smoke problems internally before they become rebate events), and never promising more than the system can chew.

**Pop quiz:** someone says "we violated our SLA." What do they mean? Almost certainly they mean *a missed SLO* — a default-settings human missed the error budget target, not a legal breach. (Correct answer, every time. You're welcome.)

## The percentile trap (the part that saves your reputation in meetings)

Never, ever manage by **average**. Here's why, with one chart-able story:

A restaurant serves 100 tables an evening. 99 are served in 30 seconds. 1 table waits 40 minutes because the waiter got lost. *Average* wait: ~54 seconds. Looks great! Meanwhile the unlucky table has already published a hellish review. **The average hid the worst experience entirely.**

Systems are exactly this. Latency isn't one number; it's a **distribution** — most requests are fast, a long tail is disastrously slow, and a single sick server can silently corrupt the average while wrecking the 99th percentile. So SRE reasons in **percentiles**:

- **p50 (median):** the typical user.
- **p95:** the grumpy-but-common user.
- **p99 / p99.9:** the user on hotel wifi. If you fix p99, you've fixed everyone below it by definition.

**2026 spin:** the same logic applies to *token* latency, *agent* turn times, and *model* response distributions — and dangerously so. Averaging an LLM's latency hides the p99 users whose requests prompted a re-generation or a timeout. Instrument at p95/p99. Always.

## Good and bad aggregation hygiene (yes, hygiene)

- Weather data over a 15-minute window hides 5-second spikes. **Know what your window hides.**
- "Average requests per second" conceals bursts. **Measure the percentile, not just the mean.**
- Your data is *not* normally distributed. Never assume it is, or your alerting will scream at ghosts and miss real monsters. (Backward lesson from the original book that AI has now made *worse* — AI outputs are wildly non-normal.)

## The control loop: your SLO should *drive* action

An SLO without a control loop is jewelry. The loop is four steps, and it's the same loop whether the executor is a human, a script, or an agent:

1. **Measure** the SLIs.
2. **Compare** to SLOs → decide if action is needed.
3. **Diagnose** — figure out what to do (e.g., "latency climbing toward budget; hypothesis: CPU-bound; options: scale out").
4. **Act** — then measure again.

In 2026, step 1 is increasingly *agentic* (*"this anomaly looks unlike your baseline"*), and step 3 is often proposed by an AI SRE ("evidence chain: config deploy at 14:02 correlated with the latency shift; suggest rollback") with a human approving step 4 when stakes are high. Keep this skeleton and let whoever is best at each step take it.

## SLOs set expectations — and the 2026 twist

Publishing SLOs manages the *other* side of trust: users (and AI agents!) build expectations on your actual behavior. **The agents' twist:** when your agent decides whether to rely on a dependency, it reads its *measured* reliability, not your marketing page. Your over-engineered service promised 5 nines and delivered them → your agents become dependent on your immortality → see the Chubby story. **Under-promise slightly, over-deliver within reason, and let your dependencies assume you're mortal.**

## The 2026 extension: judgment SLOs (preview)

By now, any veteran is muttering "but what about *output quality*?" Right. For AI systems, "alive at p99" doesn't mean "trustworthy." So in [Chapter 24](part4/ch24/) we extend the family with **judgment SLOs** ("fewer than 5% of agent tasks need human override", "policy compliance ≥ 99%", "tool-call accuracy ≥ 97%") and **safety SLIs** — behavioral truth meters for the machines that reason. The scaffold is *exactly* this chapter's scaffold; only the sensors change.

## Do this before anything else

1. Write down your users' *three* most precious promises. (Speed. Data safety. Correct answers.)
2. Turn each into one SLI + one SLO, with a window and a method. Publish it internally.
3. Compute the error budget. Announce it. Argue about everything above the line, never about the line itself.
