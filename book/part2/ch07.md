# Chapter 7 — Monitoring: Learn to See Your System

A system you can't see is a system you can't save. Monitoring is how you get eyes. And it has one golden rule, stated best by the original book and truer than ever in 2026:

> **Monitoring must never require a human to interpret anything. Software does the interpreting; humans are notified only when action is needed.**

## The three (and a half) kinds of monitoring output

Not everything deserves a pager. The classic taxonomy, which is still the law:

1. **Alerts** — a human must act *now*. Something is happening or about to happen, and your thumbs are needed.
2. **Tickets** — a human should act *eventually*. The system can't resolve it, but nothing burns down if it waits a day. (2026: often an agent files and even *pre-resolves* these.)
3. **Logging** — recorded for the record. Nobody reads logs until something prompts them to. (2026: agents read logs all the time; your logs are now *diet* for AI.)
4. **(The half) Dashboards** — a place to go when curiosity strikes. Not alerting; see-to-know.

**The structural sin:** email-alert-everything. A wall of 73 "possible anomaly" emails teaches humans to ignore email, then the one real alarm drowns. If everyone's paging you about everything, nobody's paging you about anything.

## The classic monitoring vocabulary (keep it)

- **White-box vs black-box.** *White-box:* look *inside* — CPU, queue depth, error counts. Deliciously specific, says *why*. *Black-box:* test from the outside — "the user's exact journey from a probe that looks like a user." Catches what internals miss (DNS, routing, weird clients). You need **both** (2026: black-box for agent behavior too — an agent can be internally "healthy" (200 OK) while producing nonsense; black-box reads the *output*).
- **Symptoms vs causes.** Alert on *symptoms* (users hurt) more than *causes* (a subsystem looks sad). Symptom-first keeps you honest about what users actually care about.
- **RED / USE / golden signals.** Handy starter packs: *Rate, Errors, Duration* for request-services; *Utilization, Saturation, Errors* for resources; and the classic four golden signals: **latency, traffic, errors, saturation.** Pick your container and fill it with three to seven sensors.
- **The four golden signals, translated:** ① How long did it take? (latency) ② How many users? (traffic) ③ How many failed? (errors) ④ How full is the bucket? (saturation/queue/CPU). Saturation, not "is it up," is where disasters *start*, and it earns a dedicated chapter ([Chapter 18](part3/ch18/)).

## The 2026 monitoring upgrade: three things changed

### 1. Anomaly detection replaces "95% of thresholds you wrote by hand."
Static thresholds were always a lie you told at 2 a.m. ("surely CPU at 92% is bad"). AI monitors *your actual normal*: a model watches your telemetry's rhythm and flags "this is unusual **for your system**, not for every system." Google's SRE AI (e.g., TimesFM-style forecasting) does exactly this, including reading *non-telemetry* signals like customer feedback. The pain you saved: no more alert storms on globally-defined thresholds that ignore your seasonality. The lesson you must keep: **anomaly ≠ incident; alerts say "different," agents and humans still decide "dangerous."** AI-anomaly-detection is a *first-stage filter*, not a verdict.

### 2. OpenTelemetry became the lingua franca — and now covers AI.
The industry converged on **OpenTelemetry (OTel)** as the standard way to emit metrics, logs, and traces, so you're not vendor-locked and your agents speak the common tongue. The GenAI semantic conventions (standardizing how LLM calls, agents, tool calls, tokens, etc., are traced) are the 2026 must-know: Datadog, Honeycomb, New Relic, Langfane, LangChain, CrewAI, and friends all emit OTel-compliant AI spans. If your AI stack isn't emitting OTel, you're flying an instrument at night without instruments. Full treatment: [Chapter 25](part4/ch25/).

### 3. Agents now read and react to monitoring in real time.
The 2026 architecture isn't "dashboards + humans." It's **dashboards + humans + an AI first responder** that:
- sees an anomaly or page,
- gathers logs/traces/metrics plus deployment history,
- correlates ("config deploy at 14:02 matched the latency rise"),
- and arrives at a human's phone as a *dossier with a proposed remediation* — 5-minute resolutions replacing 45-minute archaeology (vendor-reported; treat the specific numbers with salt, the *shape* is real).

## Your monitoring checklist (use it or lose it)

1. **Golden-signal coverage** on every service's user-experience path (latency/traffic/errors/saturation), white-box + black-box.
2. **Three outputs, three routes:** urgent → pager with context; someday → ticket system; rest → logs. Never "everyone email alert."
3. **Percentile-based alert views** (see Chapter 5 — mean-managed monitoring lies).
4. **A baseline:** you should be able to say what "normal" looks like, so anomaly detection (or its heir, the AI SRE) has something to be wrong against.
5. **Drillable paths:** every alert carries a link from symptom → logs → trace → recent changes. The person (human or agent) at 3 a.m. has *one hop* of context, not six.
6. **2026 add-ons:** OTel GenAI spans on all LLM/agent traffic; token and context stats; anomaly detection in front of humans; an AI triager that groups related alerts into incidents; and **monitoring of the monitors** (alerts that never fire are lying to you).

## The eternal truth

The purpose of a monitoring system is *not* to produce dashboards. It's to produce **early, cheap answers** — *is the system keeping its promises, and if not, where is the evidence pointing?* In 2026, the humans are often the last reader, not the first. Design your telemetry for the agents that stand watch tonight, and the dashboards paint themselves.
