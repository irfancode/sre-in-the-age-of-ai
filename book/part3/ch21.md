# Chapter 21 — The 2026 Launch Checklist

The classic book's launch chapter was a masterclass in *adult supervision*: launches fail for predictable reasons, so launch reviews exist to make the predictable expensive-to-skip. The original "launch checklist" (Appendix E) asked a company to prove it had SLOs, capacity, monitoring, rollback, the works, *before* the drumroll.

Here is the 2026 edition: the same heart, plus the questions that AI actually makes necessary (and note — the classic list is *more* necessary now, because AI accelerates time-to-launch and time-to-embarrassment):

## The classic checklist (still load-bearing)

1. **Define the SLO & error budget BEFORE the launch.** What promise are we making? What's our allowed-failure allowance that this launch's surprises are drawn from? (Real teams can spend a *month's* error budget in launch day — budget for it consciously.)
2. **Capacity, proven, not hoped.** Load-tested against the *launch-day* shape: announcements spike, spikes come in threes (social → news → competitor parody). Have headroom for the crest and the aftermath.
3. **Monitoring for the launch's *specific* experience**, at p95/p99, black-box *and* white-box, with dashboards that a 3 a.m. newbie (human or agent) can read.
4. **Rollback, rehearsed in the calm.** The one-command undo that actually undoes — not the "let's revert and see" that is not a plan. For multi-component launches: the *points of no return* table (what can't be undone once done — data migrations, model retrains, irreversible access grants) and what you do *at* each one.
5. **Phased rollout** — canary → ramp → full, gated by SLOs (Chapter 9), with humans aware that each phase is a *commitment checkpoint*, not a scenic view.
6. **Go/No-Go owners named.** Whose call is "stop"? (Chapter 14's incident commander can't be improvising their job at the launch party.)
7. **Launch-communication choreography** — status pages, escalation paths, and the "if you see X, do Y" compass already drafted.
8. **The post-launch review date booked** — with the outage ledger and error-budget accounting (Chapter 16) ready to be stared at honestly.

## The 2026 additions (the AI-specific gates)

If the launch involves **LLMs, agents, or model-built features** (it probably does), add:

9. **Evict the "evals or it didn't happen" gate.** A golden-set eval (quality, refusal-behavior, safety) *passed against a versioned eval set*, including regression checks vs. the previous model/prompt. "It felt better" is not an eval. (Chapter 23.)
10. **Latency & cost budget for the AI path.** Model calls add *p95* latency and *per-token* cost. Number both before launch: your error budget must include the model tier's latency, and your finance team must see the token-prediction. Saturation of the model tier is a launch-day risk (Chapter 18): can your fallback handle a provider outage?
11. **Judgment SLOs live.** If an agent will *act*: its safety SLIs (policy compliance, override rate, tool accuracy) are defined, monitored *from day one*, and wired to an autonomy budget (Chapter 24). Do not ship an unchecked actor and discover its quality three incidents in.
12. **Memory & context hygiene for the new feature.** What state does the agent/model accumulate? Versioned? Refreshable? (Chapter 19 — memory is state.)
13. **The prompt-injection & supply-chain audit.** Where does the model's input come from (injectable?), where do its tools point (compromisable?), who was the retraining data's provenance? (Chapter 29.)
14. **Agent capability rollout has its own canary.** New tools/scopes for an agent = a release: sandbox → shadow → canary → fleet, gated by live safety SLIs with auto-rollback (Chapter 8's autonomy ladder).

## When the answer is "no" to #15 (the meta-gate)

15. **"Do we know what 'good' looks like for this launch?"** If nobody can articulate the answer in one sentence, the launch isn't ready. Speed isn't the goal; *safe* speed is. The 2026 version of this is urgent because AI lets teams reach "ready to launch" at 10× the speed of discerning whether they *should*.

## The beginner's launch drill

Practice the *shape* of a launch review with something small and safe (a blog post is a sham launch, and that's fine): write the promise (SLO), predict the failure modes, plan the rollback (git revert!), schedule the review. Do it three times and the checklist stops being a form and starts being a reflex — which is exactly what it was designed to become.
