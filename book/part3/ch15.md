# Chapter 15 — The Postmortem: An Autopsy Without Blame

Most companies learn from failure in the way a child learns from a hot stove: loudly, privately, and with a scar they tell everyone about but never systematize.

The postmortem is the exception. It is the discipline's single most cult-classic practice, and it's the one I'd smuggle into any company that asks for just one.

## What a postmortem actually is

A written analysis, following a significant incident, that answers three questions, in order:

1. **What happened?** — A precise, timeline'd account. Not "the DB broke." "At 14:02 config X shipped; at 14:07 latency p95 crossed objective; at 14:09 users complained; at 14:31 rollback R deployed; at 14:52 recovery confirmed."
2. **Why did it happen?** — Contributing factors and root causes, including the *systemic* ones: monitoring gaps, missing tests, an under-documented dependency, an untested prompt. **A root cause is a starting point, not the finish line**. The mediocre postmortem finds "the bug." The good one finds "the process that let the bug through." The great one finds "the process that let the process happen" — culture, incentives, contracts.
3. **What do we change so it doesn't hurt us again?** — Action items, each with an owner and a date, tracked to completion like a release.

## The two laws of postmortems (violate at your own cost)

**Law 1 — Blameless, always.**
The investigation asks "what in the *system* failed", never "who failed." It's not softness: it's *epistemics*. When humans fear punishment, they hide evidence, soften timelines, and dodge causes — and the postmortem rots into a political memory instead of a scientific one. The paradox, proven in a decade of practice: **blameless postmortems find more true root causes, because witnesses stop being witnesses and become investigators.**

**Law 2 — Postmortem *everything significant*, whether or not it paged.**
The events that *didn't* page are often the most valuable: they expose monitoring gaps. (Nothing teaches "your alarm system is blind" like an outage nobody got paged about.)

## The 2026 update — the postmortem with an AI co-writer

Here's the good news for novices: **the postmortem is now the easiest entry-point into professional SRE, because your AI drafts it.** In production stacks (Google SRE AI auto-generates incident insights; several agents draft postmortem documents from the live incident timeline), the modern flow is:

1. **The scribe already exists** — during the incident, the live timeline was auto-captured (Chapter 14's agent note-taker). The postmortem's "what happened" section writes itself from receipts.
2. **The agent drafts structure** — timeline, contributing factors, blast radius, action-item extraction — from logs, changes, and its own investigation notes.
3. **The human edits with a red pen** — and this is the *non-negotiable human step*: the analysis of *culture and incentives* (root cause #2 and #3), the "what should have caught this earlier," and the *judgment about what to actually fix first*. An AI that detects the technical causes will happily miss "our dependency policy rewarded the risky choice" — that's your department.
4. **The actions get tracked to done.** A postmortem without tracked closure is a diary.

### Watch the automation trap
Agent-drafted postmortems have a failure mode worth naming: **they optimize for completeness of form, not truth of insight.** A beautiful, 40-item "contributing factor" salad from an LLM is a hallucination of rigor. Keep the human red pen for: *is this the real why? did we miss the systemic layer? are these five fixes actually the survivable ones?*

## The postmortem template (beginner-grade, 2026-flavored)

```
INCIDENT: [name + severity + dates]
STATUS:  [draft / in-review / fully-reviewed / actions-tracked]

1. WHAT HAPPENED
   – User impact and blast radius (who felt it, how much)
   – Timeline (from agent live-log, then edited by humans)
   – Detection path (how did we notice? monitoring? user? black-box?)

2. WHY IT HAPPENED
   – Technical root causes (+ evidence chain for each)
   – Systemic factors (monitoring, testing, dependency policy, incentives)
   – Related prior incidents (is this a repeat?)

3. WHAT WE'LL CHANGE
   – Fix  | owner | date | how we'll verify it worked
   – Both immediate hardening AND the systemic preventer

4. SCOREBOARD (optional but honest)
   – MTTR | detection time | error-budget spent | would-agent-have-caught-this
```

## Two extra practices that belong in your team

1. **Outage-hours tracking.** Record: detection time, response time, resolution time, and error budget consumed, per incident. The *trends*, not the one-offs, are the management dashboard. (Chapter 16 has the full "tracking outages" treatment.)
2. **"Postmortem about the postmortem."** Quarterly: are our incidents recurring? Are our action items actually shipped? Are we getting *better at learning*? Meta-measurement is how the discipline compounds.

## The beginner's gift to yourself

Commit to writing **one real postmortem this month** — it doesn't need to be a corporate meltdown. It can be: "my personal site went down Tuesday." Structure it like the template above. Notice two things: (a) how much better you understand the system after writing it, and (b) how the *process* of writing reveals causes you'd never have named in a group chat. That second one is the whole religion.
