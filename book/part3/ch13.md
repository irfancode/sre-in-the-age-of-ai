# Chapter 13 — Troubleshooting: CSI for Computers

The classic book said something that still reads like a koan:

> **"If a problem is hard, you'll only solve it with fast and systematic exploration of hypotheses ... but many problems don't obey this."**

Troubleshooting is a scientific method for production. You are a detective; the system is the crime scene; the clues are logs, traces, metrics, and — in 2026 — the *reasoning of a model that read them faster than you*. Let's give you the method, since method beats greasing every time.

## The method (six steps, no cheating)

1. **Fix the symptom first.** In production, *you don't troubleshoot on top of a burning house*. If users are being hurt, first protect them: throttle, redirect, rollback, restart — *mitigate* before you *diagnose*. (Mitigation isn't cheating; it's triage.)
2. **Confirm the problem.** Read the actual error, not the copy-paste of "it's broken." What *specifically* is failing, when, for whom, at what percentile?
3. **Form hypotheses.** For each, *design a test that would disprove it*. This is the entire professional skill in one sentence.
4. **Gather the evidence you didn't have to fetch by hand.** Logs, traces, dashboards, deployment history. In 2026, delegate aggressively: an AI first-responder can gather this in parallel within minutes and present a correlation, while you think about *which* hypothesis explains it.
5. **Test your top hypothesis cheaply.** One experiment at a time. (Changing three things at once "to save time" is how geniuses turn incidents into tragedy — you'll never learn which thing fixed it, and the next occurrence outruns you.)
6. **Verify the fix, then look for sisters.** Did it actually recover? Is there a second, subtler version of this failure lurking (the correlation-shaped one)? Then write the playbook entry & postmortem so this *exact* hour never repeats.

## The troubleshooting pitfalls (each with a 2026 upgrade)

| Pitfall (2017) | The 2026 upgrade |
|---|---|
| **Emotional reactions** — blaming people, panicking, "it's the network" (it's never the network) | Agents don't blame, but they *do* pattern-match confidently. Their "it's probably X" is a hypothesis needing your test, not a verdict. |
| **Willy-nilly experiment** — changing random things | Agents sometimes compensate by changing four things and claiming credit. **One experiment at a time is now a command to your AI too.** |
| **Information hiding** — clues scattered, ownership unclear | Data is *default-shared*: everyone (and every agent) sees the same dashboards and traces. |
| **Going straight for the code you wrote** | The evidence chain matters more than your feelings about a commit; agents win correlation battles (deployment history + telemetry), so let them run them — under review. |
| **Ignoring the "one second of weird"** | Anomaly detection now catches the *unusual-for-you* moments you'd have shrugged off. That shrug was the leak. |

## The 2026 first-responder model

This is how investigation actually runs in 2026 (adapt to whatever vendors you use; the shape is the same everywhere):

1. **Detection** — anomaly detector or SLO burn-rate alarm fires; also black-box probes, or a user complaint.
2. **Agent gathers** — the AI SRE pulls logs, traces, metrics, topo/dependency data, *and the last N changes*, then establishes "domain and intent": *what was this component for, what was happening, who depends on it.*
3. **Agent hypothesizes with an evidence chain** — "service X slowed at 14:02; the 14:01 config deploy enabled feature flag Y; response-time spike correlates; likely Y." Includes confidence level and *what it considered and rejected*.
4. **Human adjudicates the consequential** — approves mitigation; runs the one-cheap-experiment rule; and for reversible/low-stakes stuff, the agent (within playbook+guardrails) executes and reports.
5. **Everyone learns** — structured RCA → playbook update → auto-drafted postmortem → monitored for sisters.

Current data on the ceiling (not the halo): frontier agents score ~**38–73% diagnosis / 40–79% mitigation** on a 90-scenario high-fidelity benchmark; app-layer wins, low-level hardware/compound failure losses, and *novel* patterns collapse agent skill to ~15–28%. So: the agent is a phenomenal *gatherer and dragnet*, a sure-handed *hypothesis engine for familiar shapes*, and an *untrusted stranger on novelty*. Route novelty to humans; that's the right division of labor.

## Trust-but-verify: the one rule that carries the chapter

Troubleshooting in 2026 is **a partnership between slow thinking and fast gathering**. Your AI aligns the evidence faster than you could, and you supply the judgment it fundamentally can't: *is this the dangerous hypothesis, the expensive path, the "call the vendor" path, the "this change is worse than the failure" path?* Two habits that keep the partnership honest:

- **Require receipts.** Any agent claim arrives with *its* evidence chain. "Trust me" isn't a correlation.
- **Black-box never sleeps.** Regardless of how clever your internal telemetry is, a user-journey probe is the check that can't be fooled by your own instrumentation's blind spots. When internal signal and black-box disagree, trust the user's perspective.

## Beginner homework

1. Pick a real past incident (yours, or a public one). Rebuild the six steps from the postmortem. Where did the human *panic* step creep in?
2. Next small issue you see: *say the hypothesis out loud before acting.* Practicing "I think X, so I'll test by doing Y" 40 times makes it reflex — in you, and eventually in the agents you configure.
3. Design one "evidence-first" habit for your *own* stack: can you see "recent changes right next to the alert at 3 a.m."? If not, that's your first build.
