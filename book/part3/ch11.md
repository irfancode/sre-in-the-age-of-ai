# Chapter 11 — Practical Alerting: Make Your Phone Less Dramatic

Let's be honest about alerting: most alert systems are security theater for the ego. Ninety-five out of a hundred alerts any on-call engineer receives need no human intervention at all — industry data says only **2–5% of alerts require a real human**, and ~70% of SRE teams name **alert fatigue** a top-three operational pain. The pattern is universal, and it's a wellbeing emergency: when everything screams, soon nothing wakes anyone.

The classic book had one glorious sentence on this and I'm keeping it as the chapter's spine:

> **"The best pager is one that never goes off."**

## The alerting maturity ladder

**Level 0 — Howl at everything.** Send an email/pager for any deviation. Result: 95% noise, 5% signal, 0% trust, burned-out humans. Millions of businesses live here.

**Level 1 — Rule hygiene.** "Only page for *actionable* things." Every alert must carry: what changed, what it means, and *what to do about it* (a runbook link at minimum). If an alert can't be followed by a 3 a.m. zombie with a playbook, it doesn't page. This single discipline cuts pager volume by an order of magnitude.

**Level 2 — Symptom-based, SLO-driven.** Alert when a *user-facing promise* is about to break (the SLO's **burn rate** is burning too fast for too long), not when a knee config twitches. Attach the alert to the error budget, not the dashboard drama. This is the famous "practical alerting" upgrade, and it's where most mature teams plant their flag.

**Level 3 — The AI layer (2026).** See below.

## The four parts of a perfect alert

Whatever the level, every page should arrive as a **dossier, not a scream** (the 2026 upgrade of the classic "alert with a link to the runbook"):

1. **The what** — the SLI flinching and by how much (with a *percentile* view, naturally).
2. **The who/what's affected** — scope: users, services, regions.
3. **The why-so-far** — anomaly-detection note, correlated recent changes, candidate root cause.
4. **The do-this-next** — runbook link, proposed remediation (2026: from the AI first-responder), escalation path.

An alert that arrives with the trace, the last ten agent decisions, and a proposed fix resolves ~5× faster than a bare alarm — and it's the *only* kind that deserves a human at 3 a.m.

## The 2026 update — AI changes the game (and your job)

Three serious upgrades have landed, and your alerting design should chase all three:

1. **Anomaly detection instead of static thresholds.** A model learns *your* normal (seasonal traffic, weekly rhythms) and pings only what's *unusual for you*. Static thresholds become a fallback, not the primary brain. Big win; one caveat in the box below.

2. **Alert grouping & triage.** An AI triager folds 40 related alerts into *one incident with a reason string* ("all 40 alertions trace to checkout-db latency after the 14:02 config deploy"). Humans see incidents, not alerts. When companies deploy this, the *number of alerts humans review* collapses — the noise disappears into the dossier.

3. **Autonomous handlers on the cheap end.** An AI alert-handler can auto-address / mitigate the *recurring, well-understood*, low-stakes class of alerts (restart the flaky job, retry the batch, re-run the idempotent pipeline) and **only escalate** the puzzling or high-stakes ones. This is the correct shape of "AI took care of it" — bounded, reversible, audited, and *absolute-90% of alerts tonight would have anyway*.

### The caveats box (read this twice)

- **Anomaly ≠ incident.** "Different" is not "dangerous." Keep a human/agent layer that decides *severity*; don't let novelty page-storm you.
- **Monitor the monitors.** Alerts that never fire are lying to you. Scheduled reviews of "what alerts did we define, which fired, which were wrong/correct" — the alert *audit* — is a career-long habit worth starting young.
- **False-quiet is worse than false-alarm.** An anomaly detector that never pages during a meltdown is a morale corpse. This is why you keep *black-box* user-journey probes: they can't be fooled by your own blind spots.

## The four health warnings on your dashboard

Whatever creative instrument panel you build, keep four vitals in view (the "golden signals" from [Chapter 7](part2/ch07/)):

1. **Latency** — how long did the user wait? (percentiles!)
2. **Traffic** — how many users, and did the shape change?
3. **Errors** — how many failed, what kind?
4. **Saturation** — how stuffed are the buckets (CPU, memory, queue, connections, *tokens*, *context*)?

If you can't see in one glance whether today is a normal Tuesday or a Tuesday of doom, your dashboard is decorative. Make it interrogable — by you, and by your agents.

## On-call hygiene, the beginner edition

- **Two events per shift is the healthy target.** More → you're drowning in noise or fires; fewer → someone's hiding signals. Actually *check* the rate quarterly.
- **Every page gets a postmortem one-liner** if it was interrupt-y. What should have caught it earlier? What should have auto-handled it?
- **The pager belongs to the right people.** In 2026 that's "humans + a first-responder agent." If your agent can't explain *its* triage, it's not a first-responder, it's a noise machine with good marketing.

## Your alerting homework

1. Categorize your current pages for two weeks. Sort by: *actionable, noise, self-healable*. Publish the ratio. Shame is an excellent refactoring tool.
2. Delete or convert 80% of your "info" alerts to tickets/logs/dashboards.
3. For every remaining page: add the dossier fields (what/scope/why-so-far/do-this) — or demote it.
4. Pick one class of recurring low-stakes alert; hand it to an agent with an explicit playbook and an audit log; measure.
