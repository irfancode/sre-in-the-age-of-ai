# Chapter 14 — Emergency Response & Incident Command

Two classic chapters deserved to marry: *Emergency Response* (moving fast under fire) and *Managing Incidents* (the organizational choreography when the fire is company-wide). Here they are, one ceremony, 2026 edition.

## Part one: Emergency response — the physics of fast

Reliability, at the sharpest level, is a ratio:

> **Reliability ≈ how long until it breaks (MTTF) × how fast you fix it (MTTR).**

You can't always make the first number big. So engineering works the second: **shrink the time-to-return.** Three levers, exactly as they've always been:

1. **Pre-written playbooks.** The classic book measured a **~3× MTTR improvement** for a playbook-wielding responder vs. a "winging it" genius. Playbooks make 3 a.m. Tuesday-flavored; the measurable effect is real. In 2026, playbooks also feed your agents — *both* shifts get the cheat-sheet.
2. **Practice under pressure.** Drills, Game Days, tabletop incidents ("Wheel of Misfortune"), chaos exercises: rehearsal compresses hours into reflex. AI-era bonus: you can rehearse *agent* responses on simulated incidents and benchmark them (SREGym-style) the way pilots rehearse in simulators.
3. **Humans add latency; remove the humans from the boring parts.** The original book noted systems that *avoid asking humans* achieve higher availability even while "failing" more often — because the human hop is the expensive one. In 2026, that's the philosophical license for **agents that self-mitigate the bounded, reversible, well-understood failure class**, leaving only the consequential for humans.

### The emergency-response hierarchy (read carefully, it's the whole chapter)
1. **Prevent** (design, testing, chaos).
2. **Detect fast** (anomaly + black-box).
3. **Mitigate before you understand** (rollback, redirect, throttle — *the fix that saves the night is the one that removes the symptom first*).
4. **Diagnose, verify, and prevent recurrence** (postmortem, playbook, product-fix).
5. **Automate/autonomate the whole loop for the repeat offenders.**

## Part two: Managing incidents — the org choreography

Big incidents are organizational: *people, roles, communication, decisions*. The classic chapter's best gift was the **incident command structure**, which in 2026 is one of the rare things your AI should *augment but never replace*.

### The roles (and who/what fills them)
- **The Incident Commander (human always):** owns the incident, coordinator-in-chief, hands-off-the-knobs. Deliberately NOT the most technical person in the room — that's a trap; the commander must stay at the altitude of *decisions and communications*, not logs. Arguing-from-the-likottia is a leadership failure named every postmortem.
- **The Operations Lead (human or, with guardrails, agent):** the hands on the system, running mitigation steps.
- **The Communications Lead:** spoke-person to stakeholders, users, leadership. (2026: often partially drafted by AI from the incident timeline—still human-reviewed.)
- **The planner/tasking person:** takes "I need X" from the ops lead and makes the ask to the right team (DBAs, networking, the model provider) so the commander isn't a phone tree.
- **Note-taker / scribe (2026: an agent, usually):** live timeline — what was tried, what happened, what changed. The transcript is the raw material of the postmortem. Do not skip the scribe.

### The 2026 orchestration layer (agentic incident management)
Modern stacks (Google SRE AI's "IMAG orchestration," PagerDuty SRE Agent, etc.) add a layer *under* the human command structure:

- **Detection & enrichment** — incidents arrive grouped, enriched with scope, related changes, and confidence-forward hypotheses.
- **Live timeline** — every action (by human or agent) auto-logged; splash-proof updates to stakeholders.
- **Agent investigators** — assigned hypotheses to check in parallel ("is it the DB? is it the cache? is it the network shard?"), reporting back evidence chains instead of vibes.
- **Suggested mitigations ranked** — from "already reversed and confirmed" to "requires human approval."
- **Status machine** — the incident lifecycle (detected → updating → resolved → postmortem-pending) now manages *agents'* autonomy too: while severity is high, agent write-access narrows; when resolved, budgets recalculate.

Design rule, tested across every serious implementation: **agents gather, humans command.** The commander role is deliberately human because its essence is judgment, blame-absorption, and stakeholder trust — and no one wants to explain to the board that an agent decided to shut down checkout. (Chapter 27 has the full autonomy-gates discussion.)

## The four commandments of incident management (2026)

1. **Commander is a role, not a rank** — rotate it, train it, and (small miracle) people get *better* at it with practice. Your agents deserve real command too: practice them on simulated incidents before the night it matters.
2. **Pre-planned call path beats phone-book heroics** — escalation is designed *before* the fire. Know your secondary, your vendors, your model-provider hotlines *in the calm*.
3. **Every incident owns its learning** — captured in the same breath it's resolved: *what did we not foresee? what's the playbook delta? what needs a code fix?* Blameless, forever.
4. **The incident state is a document, not a memory** — public, live, shared: who, what, when, current-steps, owner. It's the anchor that stops the spiral into rumor and panic.

## The one slide that stays on the wall

```
MIDNIGHT RULES — for humans and agents alike
1. Reduce risk to users FIRST (mitigate > understand).
2. Commands never touch the knobs of the game they referee.
3. One experimental change at a time.
4. Timeline, not transcript; receipts, not opinions.
5. The postmortem is the deliverable; the fix is the feature.
6. Blameless, always. Agents too.
```
