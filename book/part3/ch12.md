# Chapter 12 — Being On-Call (With AI Doing the 3 a.m. Rounds)

Somewhere between the marketing decks and the conference talks, the discipline of SRE has a beating human heart: **someone is awake, and something paged them.** On-call is the moment all the theory becomes personal. So let's get it right.

## What on-call is *for*

The point of the pager is not heroism; the point is **bounded responsibility**:

- A sharp escalation policy: page the right tier, escalate to more senior humans if not resolved in X minutes.
- A promised response time (e.g., "ack in 5 minutes, resolved in 20 or you escalate").
- A *balanced* rotation so the load is shared and burnout is designed-out from the start.
- A definition of when the pager rings at all: when a *user-facing promise* is at risk — the SLO burn-rate alarm from [Chapter 11](part3/ch11/), not "one pod hiccuped." (That's the machine's problem; [Chapter 2](part1/ch02/) promised you systems that self-heal.)

## The healthy on-call cadence (the numbers so many ignore)

Repeat after the original book, because nobody listens the first hundred times:

- **An on-call engineer should receive at most ~2 *actionable* events per 8–12-hour shift.** More than that and they can't investigate properly, can't learn, and start doing damage-control on their own sanity. Two is the high end — one or fewer means the system (or agent) is doing its job beautifully.
- **Wounds heal faster with playbooks** — rehearsed, pre-recorded response steps cut MTTR roughly **3×** vs. "winging it." Playbooks are not shameful; they're the paperwork of a profession.
- **Practice makes the pager less scary.** Game Days, chaos drills, "Wheel of Misfortune" tabletop incidents — rehearsal is a genuine MTTR-gut for the whole team. In 2026, agentic SRE stacks abstract the rehearsal further (benchmarks, chaos suites, simulated incidents) but the human muscle memory still needs reps.

## The parachute principle (the most important paragraph in this chapter)

> **The human on-call is the parachute, not the aircraft.**

The full discipline is: *normal operations self-heal and self-repair with automation and AI; humans are the escalation of last resort for the weird, the ambiguous, the policy-crossing, the expensive.* Every operator should be able to sleep knowing the system won't page them for a pod restart it could do itself — and will page them with a *dossier* for the genuinely new.

In 2026 this has a name and a shape: **the AI first-responder runs the rotation *with* you**, in a provider stack that ranges from PagerDuty's SRE Agent (agent on the schedule, gathering signals, escalating only when its confidence is low — *"our agents know when they don't have enough data"*) to Datadog, Splunk, Google SRE AI, and open-source agents (Aurora, HolmesGPT). The mix that works: **agent triages and gathers, agent auto-handles the bounded-and-reversible, human adjudicates the consequential — and *everything* is logged and reviewed.** You are the experienced colleague; the agent is the diligent intern who never sleeps.

## The two sides of manning the pager in 2026

Good shifts aren't *fireless*; they're *well-composed*. On a healthy night:

1. **Boring is beautiful.** The agent or automation resolved a bunch of "pod died"/"throttled batch" events on its own. You saw zero of them (you heard about them in the summary email — see below).
2. **Page = surprise = a new playbook entry.** Every page you did receive teaches something: a monitoring gap, a missing auto-handler, a brittle dependency. Write it down *that night* while the shape is vivid.

And the hygiene that makes on-call survivable and governable:

- **Daily blameless report, the "handover of shame/receipts":** events overnight, who/what resolved them, what's still open, what to watch today. In 2026 this handover is *drafted by the agent* from its own audit trail and reviewed by the human. The review is the governance; don't let the draft be the whole company memory.
- **The 50% rule still guards your head.** If on-call work physically exceeds ~50% of your team's week, that's a *system* failure, not a toughness test. Raise it as an engineering problem, not a badge of honor.

## For the beginner: your on-call coming-out party

- **Learn the history first.** Shadow one full cycle before you hold the pager yourself. Watch the playbooks being *used*, not read.
- **Always have the ejection seat listed.** Before any shift, print/lock in: who is my secondary, who is my manager, at what point do I escalate, what is *never* worth doing myself at 3 a.m.?
- **Your prompts/playbooks are your co-pilot.** The 2026 on-call engineer writes *passing-grade instructions* — for their teammates *and for their agents*. A great playbook is one an intern (human or otherwise) can run from start to finish and land safely.
- **After every significant page: one sentence of learning.** That's the whole legacy. The company's reliability blog is just a thousand one-sentences-Learned assembled bravely.
