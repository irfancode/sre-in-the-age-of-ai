# Chapter 35 — The 90-Day Novice-to-SRE Roadmap

Here's the promise this book was built for: **in 90 days, a curious beginner can go from "I'm not sure what a server is" to running (and improving) a small production service, and being genuinely employable as a junior reliability engineer in the age of AI.** No 5-year CS degree required — the tools of 2026 are the assistant you didn't have in 2016.

This chapter is a *plan*, not a pile of links. Adapt the tool choices to your cloud of choice (or none at all — a laptop works for almost everything here). The *skills* are the map; the tools are just signposts.

## The mindset first (read once, believe it)

1. **SRE is a set of questions, not a set of machines.** "What promise are we making? How do we measure it? What happens when it breaks? How do we learn?" — you can practice all four with a lemonade stand.
2. **Your AI is your study partner, not your answer key.** Every line it writes, you must be able to explain *why*. The exercise is verification (Chapter 28) — you're literally training the skill you'll be paid for.
3. **Boring beats impressive.** Build the boring, reliable thing. The "impressive" projects are where careers get 2 a.m. incidents.

## The 90 days

### Days 1–15: The basics (see the machine)
**Goal:** genuinely understand *what software systems are* — from your laptop to a web service.

- Make a website "server" of your own: a plain folder served to your phone over the home wifi. Watch the request show up in a log file. *(Congratulations: you've now seen a request, and a log.)*
- Break and fix things on purpose, locally: stop the process and restart it; kill it harder; make a script that restarts it. *(You're now automating healing — Chapter 8's rung 2.)*
- Read Chapter 2 and the glossary (Appendix B) and *annotate them* with your own experiment results. Annotated learning is remembering.

*AI teammate task:* ask it to explain your own log file to you and then *disprove its explanation* with the actual file. That's the single best "verify your copilot" exercise that exists.

### Days 16–30: The measuring (numbers over moods)
**Goal:** learn what "keeping a promise" means numerically — SLIs, SLOs, error budgets.

- Give your tiny service a promise: "my page loads in under 1 second, 95% of the time." Measure it (curl + a cron + a spreadsheet counts). You now own an SLI and a provisional SLO (Chapter 5).
- Deliberately violate it (comment out a cache; add a sleep) and catch yourself violating it. *(Detection practice — Chapter 16's religion in miniature.)*
- Read Chapters 4–5 and Chapter 11. Compute a "nines table" (Appendix A) for your promise while you're sober and un-harassed.

*AI teammate task:* have it *review your monitoring plan* — your one metric, collected by one cron — and punch holes in it (window, aggregation, black-box hole?). Adopt at least one improvement; reject at least one with a reason. *The rejecting-with-a-reason is the actual skill.*

### Days 31–45: The running (on-call for your own system)
**Goal:** practice the response habits. The pager discipline, at zero risk.

- Take one real on-call week on your own service: rotate the "night on-call" with yourself; when the alarm (cron + desktop-notification) fires, run it like a real incident: timeline, hypothesis, playbook, postmortem (Chapters 12–15).
- Write *one real postmortem* for your worst self-inflicted failure, using the Chapter 15 template. Purge the first draft with your AI; then re-edit *it* with your red pen — edit, don't accept.
- Set up a basic **incident file template** that a stranger could pick up. (That document is your insurance policy.)

*AI teammate task:* hand it the incident and ask it to draft the postmortem *from the timeline only*; then find the three things it invented. (It will invent. This is the lesson.)

### Days 46–60: The automation (make it repair itself)
**Goal:** port the classical toil-killers to your own stack (and your own computer).

- Automate *your own* repeat-from-the-word-automate moments (a restart script; an "is it up?" check; a "why am I so slow" probe) — the toil ledger from Chapter 6, in miniature.
- Introduce a *second piece* (a tiny API behind your site) and a load condition that breaks it; make the monitoring catch it; make the rerun/stxor-replay safe (idempotency — Chapter 16).
- Script your deploy: one command that rebuilds and re-serves your site; your *rollback* command rehearsed in the calm (Chapter 9).

*AI teammate task:* ask it to write the deploy script *and* the rollback script; then review both line-by-line for security and reversibility (Chapters 29 / 21 are your rubric). Your review, your signature, your responsibility — that's the deal.

### Days 61–75: The AI systems (your first judgment SLOs)
**Goal:** get your hands on real AI reliability — the 2026 differentiator.

- Add *one* AI feature to your world (a small summarization or classification task you care about): 
  — ship it with **an eval set** (10–20 golden examples, versioned — Chapter 23);
  — measure **latency, tokens-per-task, and cost** per call (Chapters 23 & 25);
  — define **one judgment SLO** ("fewer than 1 in 10 outputs needs correction") and hang a meter on it (Chapter 24).
- Purge a vision of the "model is a magic function" malady from Chapters 23's cautionary list — *when the model lies, catch it being wrong confidently and quietly.* Your eval set is how.

*AI teammate task:* have it build a *second* eval-set from the *first* one's failures (the drift test — Chapter 16's replay in miniature). Then have the two eval-sets disagree with each other and decide which is right yourself: judge-verdict arbitration, pure and 2026.

### Days 76–90: The portfolio (become employable)
**Goal:** turn the 90 days into *evidence a hiring manager can read* (Chapter 31 knows the profile).

- **One-week project:** a small "production-ish" service (any cloud or your laptop in a Docker container) with: an SLI/SLO table, a monitoring dashboard showing p95 + a judgment meter, a canary-and-rollback release path, one chaos drill you ran (kill your own service — Chapter 26), one postmortem, and a 2-page "runbook" an agent (or stranger) could follow.
- **A write-up (the resume substitute):** "What I built, the promises I made, how I measured them, what broke, how I learned." Publish it (GitHub Pages is free — the exact trick this book's own blog uses).
- **Talk the talk with receipts:** the vocabulary everywhere in this glossary (Appendix B) now describes *your actual work*. When you interview, you're not reciting — you're reporting.

## The honest "you'明显re-ready" checklist (self-scored)

At day 90 you should be able to say "yes" to all seven:

1. "I can describe my service's promise in one sentence." (SLO literacy)
2. "I measured it, and I know my p95." (Metrics honesty)
3. "I can run a page with a timeline and a playbook." (Incident habits)
4. "I wrote a postmortem and tracked its actions." (Learning culture)
5. "I can deploy and *rollback* in one command." (Release discipline)
6. "I have evals, token costs, and a judgment SLO for an AI feature." (The 2026 edge)
7. "I review my AI's work line-by-line, and I can explain why." (The job, in one sentence)

## The parallel career lanes (what you can aim at after day 90)

- **Platform/infra SRE** — dive deep on the cloud-native stack (tracing, chaos, capacity). Learning map: Chapters 2, 16–19 done *properly* (months, not evenings).
- **AI/LLM SRE (the new fast lane)** — double-down on 22–30: evals, agent governance, judgment SLOs. *Demand for this is 2026's most under-supplied skill, and it's the one that rewards the self-taught most.*
- **Developer-rel-engineering / release** — own the pipelines and gates of Chapter 9 at company scale.
- **Incident/chaos specialist** — the drills-and-command path (Chapters 14, 26); rare and deeply respected.

## The closing nudge

Nobody on your future team will know (care?) that 90 days ago you didn't know what a server was. They'll read your runbook, watch you hold the pager, and ask one question: *"does this person make the system — and its machines — better?"* If the answer is evidence-flavored and honest, you're an SRE. The title was never the point; the *promises* were the point, all along.
